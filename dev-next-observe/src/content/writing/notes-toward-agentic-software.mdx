_This is a working note, not a finished argument._

I've spent a large part of my career reviewing other people's code: as a co-founder setting standards for a team of a dozen engineers, as a mentor to interns, as a contractor inheriting systems I didn't design. Lately I've been reviewing a new kind of contributor — AI agents that open pull requests — and I've noticed that my review habits don't transfer as cleanly as I expected.

## What a review is actually checking

When I review a human's change, the diff is the smallest part of what I'm evaluating. I'm asking whether the change respects invariants nobody wrote down: that tenant context flows through background jobs, that money only moves through the ledger, that a migration can run on a table with millions of rows without locking it. Good engineers internalise these rules over months. They show up in review comments long before they show up in documentation.

Agents, so far, are excellent at the diff and uneven at the invariants. The code compiles, the tests pass, and the change is still wrong in a way only someone who knows the system would see.

## Benchmarks measure the diff

Benchmarks like SWE-bench were a real step forward: they evaluate agents on real issues from real repositories, judged by real tests. But tests are only the invariants someone thought to encode. A lot of what makes a production system safe lives outside them.

That suggests a research question I keep returning to: _can we evaluate agents against the invariants of a system, not just its tests?_ For a multi-tenant application, that might mean checking that no change can make one tenant's data visible to another — a property, not an example.

## Review history as training signal

The second question is about learning. Every codebase I've worked on has a review history full of comments like "this needs to go through the posting service" or "don't load the whole table here". Those comments are the closest thing a team has to a written-down architecture. Retrieving them as context for an agent editing the same module seems like an obvious experiment, and I haven't yet seen it done carefully.

## Where I'm going with this

I don't think agents will replace engineering judgement soon. I do think the most useful thing an engineer with production experience can bring to AI research is a clear account of what that judgement consists of — and better ways to measure whether a system has it. That's the work I want to do next.
